# Project Audit — "Does alignment learn semantics rather than length/syntax?"

*Date: 2026-08-29. A full review of every method tried across rounds 1–8, judged against the
actual research question rather than each round's local metric. Plain language throughout.
New data generated for this audit: an untrained-baseline evaluation (which never existed), a
length/content decomposition of all three gaps, per-example sensitivity statistics, and
cluster-robust confidence intervals. Script: `scripts/audit_all_methods.py`;
data `metrics/audit_all_methods.csv`, `metrics/length_split_fulltest_BASE_untrained.csv`.*

---

## 0. The short version

1. **You have tried ~128 training runs covering 10 published methods and 6 home-grown
   families.** That is a lot, and it is not the reason you are stuck.
2. **You are stuck because the measuring instrument is broken in three separate ways.** Not
   the methods. The three defects each independently make "g2 ≈ 0, g3 ≈ 0" mean something
   different from what you think it means.
3. **Once measured correctly, you already have a partial positive answer** — and it has been
   sitting in your results since round 6. On average, trained models show a large, consistent
   *semantic* preference and essentially **zero systematic** length or syntax preference. That
   is a real finding, and the untrained model does *not* have it.
4. **But the stronger claim you actually want is false, and no method fixes it.** Per
   individual example, the model's score moves as much when you only rewrite the *syntax* as
   it does between genuinely different meanings — for about **44% of examples**. The near-zero
   averages come from large positive and negative effects cancelling out.
5. **The single most useful thing you can do next is fix the dataset and the metric**, not
   invent method #17.

## 2. Three reasons the scoreboard has been misleading you

### 2.1 A longer sentence has a lower probability. Always. So g2 and g3 can never be 0.

`g3 = logP(rejected″) − logP(rejected)` is a **sum** over tokens. Every extra token subtracts
roughly 2.5 nats, no matter how good the text is. On the validation set `rejected″` is on
average 0.33 tokens longer than `rejected`, and the two have identical token length in only
**8%** of examples.

So even a model that is *perfectly* indifferent to length still scores g3 ≈ −1 to −1.5. Every
gap splits exactly into two parts:

```
gap = (per-token score difference) × (average length)     <- CONTENT: what you want to measure
    + (token-count difference)     × (average per-token)  <- LENGTH: unavoidable arithmetic
```

For DPO v3 the celebrated `g3 = −1.73` is **−0.19 content and −1.54 pure arithmetic**. In other
words, DPO v3's length-content effect is already essentially zero, and what remains is the
measuring stick, not the model. Chasing g3 → 0 was chasing a number that cannot reach 0.

**The correct target is not 0. It is the dataset's own length floor (≈ −1.0 for g2, ≈ −1.5 for g3).**

### 2.2 Averaging across examples hides the entire effect

This is the big one. Compare, for the *content* component:

| model | mean g2 (the "bias") | mean \|g2\| per example (the "sensitivity") |
|---|---|---|
| BASE (untrained) | −1.86 | **15.1** |
| DPO v3 | −0.97 | **19.7** |
| Dr.DPO | −0.86 | **24.9** |
| drdpo_accum | −0.98 | **26.2** |

Read that again. Dr.DPO's syntax gap *averages* −0.86 nats, which looks like beautiful syntax
invariance. But on a typical individual example, rewriting only the syntax changes the score by
**25 nats** — which is *larger* than Dr.DPO's entire semantic gap (22 nats). The average is near
zero only because roughly half the examples go one way and half the other.

And training makes the per-example sensitivity **worse**, not better: 15.1 → 24.9.

Every round-1-to-8 table reported the mean only. The mean was never the quantity that answers
your question.

### 2.3 Your evaluation set is 195 prompts, not 2000

| | rows | unique |
|---|---|---|
| validation | 2000 | **195 unique prompts** (1174 unique prompt+chosen+rejected triples) |
| train | 3000 | **524 unique prompts** |

Each prompt appears about 6–10 times with different response pairs. Statistically the
effective sample is the number of *prompts*, not rows. Re-running round 7's headline
comparison with confidence intervals clustered by prompt:

- `drdpo_i4b` vs DPO v3 on the binding subset: +0.066, 95% CI **[+0.017, +0.111]** — still real,
  but the interval is about **twice** as wide as the naive one. Good news: **the round-7 result
  survives.**
- overall accuracy: +0.011, CI [−0.009, +0.030] — not significant, which the report already said.

Smaller claims (round 6's "Dr.DPO beats DPO v3 by 0.007") are far inside the noise. There is no
leakage — train and validation share zero prompts — so this is a precision problem, not a
validity problem.

**Two smaller data issues:** 6.2% of validation rows have all three rejected variants
byte-identical (contributing an exact 0 to g2 and g3, diluting both toward zero); and the
"syntax-only" pair `rejected_prime` vs `rejected_double_prime` actually differs by **46% of its
characters** on average — these are two different paraphrases, not one sentence with its syntax
rearranged. g2 was never a clean syntax probe.

## 3. So what *is* the answer to the scientific question?

Split it into two questions, because they have different answers.

### Q1: "Does the model have a systematic preference for length or syntax?" → **No. That is a real, defensible finding.**

Using mean ÷ standard deviation across examples ("how consistent is the effect"):

| model | semantics (g1) | syntax (g2) | length (g3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| BASE (untrained) | **0.65** | −0.09 | **−0.25** |
| DPO v3 | 0.56 | −0.04 | **−0.01** |
| Dr.DPO | 0.60 | −0.03 | −0.03 |
| SimPO v2 | 0.90 | −0.06 | **−0.62** |

Trained models (except SimPO) have a strong consistent semantic effect and a *statistically
indistinguishable-from-zero* systematic syntax/length effect. The untrained model does show a
length bias (−0.25) which training removes (−0.01). **Alignment does remove systematic length
bias.** SimPO is the counterexample — it *introduces* a strong brevity bias (−0.62).

### Q2: "Is the model's judgement *robust* to length/syntax rewrites?" → **No, and nothing you tried fixes it.**

Percentage of examples where a pure syntax rewrite moves the score **more** than the actual
semantic difference does:

| model | syntax beats semantics | length beats semantics |
|---|---|---|
| BASE (untrained) | 43.8% | 36.9% |
| DPO v3 | 44.7% | 42.1% |
| Dr.DPO | 43.8% | 41.1% |
| **drdpo_i4b** (current champion) | **45.1%** | **45.6%** |
| **SimPO v2** | **32.4%** | 29.4% |
| **round-8 cf arms** | **28–31%** | — |

For nearly half of all examples the model is more moved by *how something is phrased* than by
*what it means*. Training does not improve this; the current champion makes it slightly worse.

Two exceptions, both informative:
- **SimPO** improves it (32%) because length normalization compresses per-example spread — but
  pays with the worst systematic brevity bias of any method.
- **The round-8 counterfactual method improves it most (28–31% vs 39–42% for its own controls)**,
  because it is the only method that explicitly optimizes the *worst* case within each example
  rather than the average. Caveat: most of that gain comes from raising g1 (14.8 → 19.1) rather
  than lowering syntax sensitivity (15.2 → 14.8), and it ran on a smaller training set and
  eval slice, so it is suggestive, not proven.

## 4. Which method is actually best?

There is no single winner because the project has been scoring two different games.

| Goal | Winner | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **Your stated research goal** (g1 big, g2/g3 small — content scale) | **Dr.DPO family**: `drdpo_accum`, `drdpo_rdpo`, Dr.DPO | g1 **22–23** (base 14.4), syntax bias −1.0, length bias −0.4 |
| **Length-fairness of decisions** (`worst_subset`) | **`drdpo_i4b`** | worst 0.672, length gap 0.001 — but **g1 +0.12, i.e. no semantic gain at all over the untrained model** |
| **Per-example robustness** (the thing that's actually broken) | round-8 cf arms; SimPO | 28–32% vs 39–44% |
| Overall accuracy | `drdpo_strat` 0.668 | vs base 0.601 — everything is stuck in a narrow band |

**This is the core of your confusion, and it is a real problem, not a misunderstanding on your
part.** Rounds 5, 6 and 7 progressively optimized `worst_subset` — a *decision-fairness* metric
invented in round 5 — and crowned `drdpo_i4b` as the project's best method. But measured against
the *original research question*, `drdpo_i4b` is one of the **weakest** trained models: its
semantic separation is statistically identical to the untrained base model (+0.12 nats, CI
crosses zero). The project drifted from its own question, and the leaderboard followed the drift.

If someone asks "what is your best method for the semantics-vs-length question," the honest
answer today is **Dr.DPO or `drdpo_accum`**, not `drdpo_i4b`.

## 5. The gap, stated plainly

1. **You cannot currently measure syntax sensitivity.** The g2 pair is two different
   paraphrases (46% character difference), not a syntax-controlled rewrite, and 6% of rows are
   degenerate.
2. **You have been reporting the wrong statistic.** Means cancel; the per-example magnitude is
   where the phenomenon lives, and it says the model is *not* robust.
3. **Your eval is ~195 prompts.** Everything below ~0.04 accuracy difference is noise.
4. **You never had an untrained control** until this audit, so no claim about what *alignment*
   does was ever properly grounded.
5. **The accuracy ceiling (~0.60 base → ~0.67 best) is real** and is a model/data limit, exactly
   as rounds 6–7 concluded. That part of the story holds up.

## 6. What to do — in priority order

**Step 1 — Fix the metric (1 day, no training).**
Report for every method, on the content scale: mean (bias), mean |·| (sensitivity), and
mean/sd (consistency), with prompt-clustered confidence intervals. `scripts/audit_all_methods.py`
already does this. Re-rank all 19 existing checkpoints. You may find you are done for g1 and g3
without training anything new.

**Step 2 — Rebuild the evaluation set (highest value per unit of work).**
- Many more unique prompts (target ≥2000 unique, not 195). This alone halves your error bars.
- Make `rejected_prime`/`rejected_double_prime` **token-length identical** to each other and to
  `chosen`. Currently identical only 30% / 8% of the time. This kills defect 2.1 outright.
- Make the syntax perturbation a **minimal edit** (reorder a clause, change voice) rather than a
  free paraphrase, so g2 measures syntax and nothing else.
- Drop the 6% degenerate rows.
- Generate several *independent* syntax rewrites per example, so you can measure per-example
  variance directly — that is the quantity you actually care about.

**Step 3 — Change the objective to match the question.**
Your goal "judge by meaning, not surface form" is a **robustness/invariance** goal, so the loss
should penalize the *worst* surface variant of each example, not the average. That is exactly
the shape of the round-8 Support-Augmented KL-Dr.DPO you just built, and it is already the best
thing you have on the per-example metric. With a proper dataset (step 2) it has substantially
more room: right now the three counterfactuals are so similar in difficulty that the inner
adversary barely engages (counterfactual mass 0.14 vs a nominal 0.15).

**Step 4 — Only then compare methods again.** With a fixed metric and dataset, re-run the three
survivors (Dr.DPO, `drdpo_accum`, round-8 cf) on 2–3 seeds each. Everything in this project is
currently single-seed.

**What not to do:** do not invent method #17 against the current metric. Twelve of your ~128 runs
were spent optimizing a number (`worst_subset`) that is only loosely related to your question,
and ~96 were scored with a statistic (the mean) that cancels the effect you are hunting.
